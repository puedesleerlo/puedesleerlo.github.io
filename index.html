The question

Physics, philosophy, risk, governance. Four notations, one problem.

Every role on this page is the same question written in a different notation: how should a decision be made when the evidence cannot settle it? I have been circling it since high school. It is why I chose the degrees I chose, in the order I chose them.

I · Physics — mechanisms are portable

I studied Physics Engineering to learn how systems evolve in time. What turned out to matter was not any particular system but a structural fact: the mathematics does not care about the substrate. A Markov process is indifferent to whether its state is a particle or a borrower. A diffusion equation does not know whether it is describing heat or the spread of a default.

So when I later found Markov processes and diffusion models sitting at the centre of credit risk, it read less as a surprise than as a confirmation. The mechanisms we already understand are transferable, and building real applications on top of mathematics that is already solved is itself a way of learning what decision-making is made of.

II · Philosophy — what counts as evidence

I went back for a second degree, in philosophy, to work on epistemology: Popper and falsification, evolutionary epistemology, history and philosophy of science, philosophy of mind, logic. In my second semester I ran into a decision-making course and it never let go of me. The question I cared about was narrow and stubborn — what actually counts as evidence for or against a theory? History of science supplied the other half: how decisions about technology get made, and how technologies get built out of those decisions.

My thesis took the hardest version of the problem: how do you decide about a technology when no evidence is available, no science can predict the outcome, and the potential human cost is large? My answer was that nobody can forecast the impact of a technology, so ethics and principles have to lead rather than follow the evidence — but as falsifiable commitments that are actually tested, not as a rulebook you comply with.

A principle that cannot fail is not a principle. It is decoration.

III · Risk — where it broke

Banking is where I put that to work. Credit risk is decision-making under uncertainty with the uncertainty made explicit and priced, which is as close to a laboratory as this question gets. Two things happened there.

The first confirmed the physics intuition. Models built for physical systems do describe behavioural and economic data, and the real craft is reconciling the macro world — macroeconomic variables — with the micro world — longitudinal, month-by-month client-loan tracking. Two resolutions of a single object.

The second was the pandemic, and it is the reason I am still working on this. COVID moved micro and macro behaviour in ways that did not correlate. Relief programmes hit the two levels differently — yet they were two versions of the same behaviour, produced by a single cause, and they should have been explainable together. Instead the models were evaluated the way they always were: context-free, on their metrics. Decisions were made without an account of the endogenous dynamics driving them. And where a financial explanation and a machine-learning explanation of the same outcome competed, the ML counterpart tended to win — because it carried the name of science.

Philosophy had already told me why that is wrong. Technical metrics are not conclusive once the evidence base has gone. This is the problem of induction wearing a crisis as a costume: the past has quietly stopped being a guide, and no amount of backtesting will tell you so, because backtesting is made of the past.

I answered with what I had: experimentation and simulation, backtesting coverage pushed from 20% to 90%, Monte Carlo, thousands of model runs a week. I still have not found the answer. I had to report an expected risk; the expected risk could not be honestly derived from the evidence. Decision-making under uncertainty is the correct name for the problem, but no formal framework I could find actually handles decisions this complex inside an organisation that expects evidence-based management.

IV · Governance — building the layer

That is where I stopped being only a builder. If technical decisions cannot be validated by their own metrics, then what is missing sits around them: a feedback and control loop bound to actual outcomes and aware of context, not just to metrics. A monitoring layer over technical decision-making, whatever the algorithms underneath happen to be.

I moved into public-space governance to build one, and to have the leadership and autonomy to test the idea on something real. Public space asks micro and macro questions under uncertainty constantly. I wanted a system where information was integrated and ground truth established — where you could see in real time what was actually happening, with artificial agency, explicit context, and ontologies doing the work of holding meaning steady across thirty-four incompatible historical standards.

On its own terms the project was a considerable success. The numbers are further down this page. I still did not get the answer.

V · What I don't know yet

That is the honest reason I am at Carnegie Mellon. I ran out of formal apparatus, and I needed better tools than the ones I had taught myself.

I am now fairly convinced that the relation between governance and decision-making is a genuinely technical problem, and a hard one. It involves desires, coordination and evaluation at the same time, and it does not dissolve by choosing better metrics. What I want to understand is how to build trust, and how to build environments that produce good decisions, precisely where the human cost is high and the evidence is thin.

I do not have it yet. That is what the next few years are for.
