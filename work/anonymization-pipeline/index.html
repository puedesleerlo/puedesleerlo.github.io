The Data the AI
Cannot ReachA Municipal PII Anonymization Pipeline

Privacy as an architectural property, not a policy promise — how to make personal data structurally unreachable by everything built on top.

By Mario Alejandro Tabares Arango Associate Director of Data & Technology, CORLIDE City of 2.5M residents
Above this line: the semantic layer, retrieval, agents. Below it: data no consumer can name.
I The design problem Two legal mandates and one system that must never be able to comply with the wrong one.

Access policies fail open. Absent data does not.

Three years of citizen petitions and municipal records sat in front of me, and every one of them was about a person: a name, a document number, an address precise enough to knock on. The law required the office to answer each petition within fifteen business days. It also required the office to analyse the whole corpus in order to govern public space at all. Both mandates were real, and both required the data. Above them, a third constraint: an AI layer was going to be built on this — retrieval, agents, a semantic layer — and it must never be able to reach an identity.

The conventional answer is access control. Scope the role, filter the queries, write the policy, review it quarterly. I did not trust it, for reasons that have nothing to do with the quality of anyone's policy. Access policies fail open. They fail open under pressure — a director needs an answer tonight, a permission is widened for an afternoon and never narrowed again. They fail open under misconfiguration — one grant to the wrong identity, one export written to a shared folder. And they fail open under a consumer that is, by design, inventive about reaching everything it is permitted to reach.

The only privacy guarantee an AI system upstream cannot erode is data that no longer contains the information. Not data it is forbidden to read — data that does not have the answer.

So I built the guarantee into the data rather than into the permissions around it. Guarantee by construction, not by permission. Everything below follows from that one commitment.

II Threat model Stated as patterns. Four ways a release becomes a person again.

What the pipeline is defending against

Re-identification from quasi-identifiers No direct identifier present, and yet enough incidental structure — a place, a date, an exact value — to narrow a population down to one person.
Linkage across datasets Two releases that are each harmless, joined on a column they happen to share, becoming an identity that neither contained on its own.
An over-capable or compromised consumer An agent that composes queries nobody anticipated, or one whose instructions have been shaped by the very content it retrieved.
Human error in query construction The most common failure by far, and the least dramatic: an analyst joins the wrong two tables and produces a file nobody meant to exist.

The downstream AI is treated as an untrusted consumer, deliberately. Not because I expect it to misbehave, but because a privacy guarantee that depends on a language model behaving as expected is not a guarantee. It is a forecast.

III The pipeline Read it from the bottom up. The direction is the argument.

One direction of travel

The pipeline has one job: produce a released layer safe enough to hand to anything, and make the boundary between raw and released a one-way street. The diagram is drawn the way the system is stacked — raw at the bottom, the AI layer at the top, everything flowing upward and nothing coming back down.

IV Four design decisions Each one is a bet about how the system fails, not about how it works.

Four decisions, and what each one is buying

Decision Risk it addresses What would go wrong without it

Decision A Irreversibility over encryption

Where identity must be destroyed but linkage must survive, I hash irreversibly with SHA-256 rather than encrypt or tokenize through a reversible mapping. The same person yields the same token on every run, so joins and deduplication keep working — while the value itself leads nowhere.

Risk it addresses

Encryption moves the secret rather than removing it. If a key exists, a decrypt path exists — and therefore something to steal, to escalate to, to be compelled to produce. A reversible token vault is the same exposure with a database in front of it.

Without it

The released layer would be personal data under a thin cover. Every downstream system inherits the obligation of holding personal data, and a single leaked key retroactively de-anonymizes every release ever made. Hashing has no such failure mode: there is nothing to leak, and no one — including me — can reverse a release.

Decision B One technique per attribute class

Three techniques, chosen by what an attribute is for: hashing where a join depends on it, suppression where the field carries no analytic value, generalization where the distribution matters and the individual value does not. The classification is the design work; the transformation is the easy part.

Risk it addresses

Uniform treatment, in either direction. Blanket suppression destroys the analysis the legal mandate requires. Blanket hashing preserves fields that should simply not exist downstream, and quietly turns high-cardinality attributes into near-unique keys — a re-identification vector wearing the costume of a pseudonym.

Without it

You get one of two failures, both common. Either the released layer is useless and people route around it — and the real privacy failure in a public institution is a shadow copy on somebody's laptop — or it stays expressive enough to re-identify, because everything survived in some form.

Decision C Placed below the AI layer

The transformation happens in the data layer, beneath the semantic layer, retrieval and agents. Above the boundary, isolation is double: the consumer identity holds read access to the released layer and no grant whatsoever on the restricted side, so the engine refuses even hand-written queries — and the AI workspace only ever declares released tables, so restricted models are not nameable in it. One barrier is enforcement; the other makes the mistake unwriteable.

Risk it addresses

Privacy implemented at the model boundary — system prompts, output filters, guardrails, “the agent has been instructed not to reveal.” Those are properties of a model's behaviour, and behaviour is precisely the thing you cannot bound. It is also the layer an attacker gets to write to.

Without it

The guarantee would depend on prompt design, on which model was swapped in last week, on whether a retrieved document happened to contain instructions. It would need re-validation on every model change and would degrade silently between them. Placed below, the guarantee is invariant to all of it — I can change model, framework or agent design freely, because none of them alters what is in the data.

Decision D Validation as a gate, not an audit

Every release is checked for residual identifiers and linkage risk before anything downstream can consume it, and a failure blocks the release. The gate and the transformation are written against the same definition of what counts as an identifier, so a released value that trips the gate is a defect in the transform — not a disagreement between two teams' rules.

Risk it addresses

Audit after the fact. An audit tells you that personal data was published; it cannot un-publish it. In the interval between publication and detection the data has been retrieved, embedded, cached, and quite possibly answered with.

Without it

Leaks would be found by whoever found them, on their schedule. And the case the gate actually catches is never the designed path — that one is correct by construction. It is the undesigned one: free text, where a person typed an identifier into a box meant for a description. One dataset remains unpublished today for exactly this reason. It will cross when it is transformed, and not before.

VI What I'd probe next Three things I would not defend as finished.

Where I think this is still weak

The treatment is field-level. It reasons about attributes one at a time, and the sharpest re-identification attacks do not — they work on combinations. A formal k-anonymity or ℓ-diversity style guarantee over quasi-identifier sets would say something the current design only implies.

Risk drifts even when the release does not. Re-identification risk is not a property of my release; it is a property of my release joined to every other dataset in the world, and that set keeps growing. What is safe against today's auxiliary data is not thereby safe. I have no mechanism that notices when that stops being true.

The gate encodes judgement that ought to be quantitative — release criteria argued from a risk model rather than set by an engineer who has thought about it carefully. Formalising those criteria is the piece I would build next, and the piece I would most want someone to argue with me about.

§ On what this page omits
Note on disclosure

The specific attributes transformed, the rule logic and the validation criteria are omitted from this public version by design — in a privacy system, the configuration is part of the attack surface. Publishing the map of what is treated, and how, hands a reader the shape of what is left.
