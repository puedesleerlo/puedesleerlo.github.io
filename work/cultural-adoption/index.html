Practice Before
the DeadlineTeaching a municipal office to decide with AI it should not fully trust

An analytical observatory can give a team leader an answer in minutes, but it cannot say when that answer is wrong. Team leaders practiced that judgment in timed rounds on the observatory's real outputs. A leaderboard scored how accurate their decisions were and how often they caught a mistake.

By Alejandro Tabares Associate Director of Data & Technology, CORLIDE Analytical observatory, Public Space office Project placed third in a city-wide competition

We ran the exercises with team leaders. The figures on this page come from the strategic plan, and any figure that is a target is labeled a target, not a result.

I The problem A diagnosis, and the hour before a committee.

The office had answers before it knew when to believe them

In 2026 the Public Space office of Medellín was building an analytical observatory: governed datasets, dashboards, and an AI agent that answers questions about the city's public space. The office was my client, and I led the observatory's adoption plan. I designed the technical architecture, worked out the incentives of the people who would use it, and set the strategy for bringing it into daily work. We started with a diagnosis. The office scored 1.83 out of 5 across the twelve DAMA-DMBOK knowledge areas and had 19 of the 87 required controls in place. We also asked leaders and coordinators what the program needed most. Their top three answers were all about people: better data quality and ownership, an analytical mindset for decisions, and more willingness to adopt the project.

The hardest case is a familiar one. A team leader has an hour before a committee, and the observatory's answer disagrees with the figure they remember. They can ignore the observatory and go back to a private spreadsheet. They can accept its answer because it came quickly and looks finished. Both are mistakes. We wanted leaders to learn to check an output quickly enough to still use it, and to know which kinds of output need checking.

The plan trains for that hour. Leaders rehearse the decision against a clock, on outputs that are sometimes wrong. The rest of this page explains how.

II Rehearsal A clock, a score, a guide and a helper, taken from games and teaching labs.

A clock, a score and a lab

We treated adoption as something to practice, and took the structure from games and teaching labs. The scoring needed as much care as the content. A leader who is rewarded only for agreeing with the system learns to agree with it, so the score had to reward doubt when the doubt was justified.

Two documents. The strategic plan covers the diagnosis, the roles, the four phases, the weekly habits and the metrics. The operating guides contain the exercises: their scripts, time limits and scoring. The figures on this page come from the strategic plan, and the exercises are described only in outline.

Timed decisions A leader receives a question from their own area together with the observatory's answer. Before time runs out they must use the answer, correct it, or send it back. The exercise is timed because the real decision is too.
A leaderboard Every round is scored, and the group can see the scores. Leaders know the rule before they play: they earn points for an accurate decision and for catching a wrong output. A leader who accepts everything loses points on the wrong outputs. A leader who rejects everything loses points on accuracy.
Guided exercises In the first rounds a script says what to look at: where the figure comes from, how fresh the data is, and what its quality sheet says. Later rounds give less of the script, and the leader decides what to check.
Lab assistance Someone from the transformation team sits with the group, as a lab assistant would. They answer questions, step in when the group is stuck, and otherwise watch. The plan sets in advance how much of the work the assistant does in each phase.

Where intuition about quality comes from. The exercises are practice. The plan also builds the same judgment into the working week through two habits. In the data-health check, a steward looks at freshness, volumes and failed loads, then assigns each service one of four states. In triage, the team rates the severity of every incident, and a P0 or P1 needs a root-cause analysis within 5 business days. Those analyses, together with the list of incidents that recurred within 30 days, show the team which of its earlier calls were wrong.

Where deciding under uncertainty comes from. The third habit is called use and fix. A team works with a dataset as it is and keeps its quality sheet open. It reports whatever gets in the way and repairs it in the same weekly session. Nobody waits for clean data before deciding. The team learns how far the data it has can be relied on.

III Fading help The helper's share is written down before the first session and only goes down.

Help that is scheduled to leave

Assistance is the mechanic most likely to backfire, because a team that always has an expert in the room learns to ask the expert. The plan therefore sets how much of the operation the transformation team carries in each phase. It also gives the internal team a growing share of incidents and changes to resolve alone. A team moves to the next phase only when it meets those criteria, however much time has passed.

Two tracks
Functional teams and technical teams go through the same four phases in parallel and synchronize at set points.
A speed check
By phase 3 the internal team should resolve an incident in no more than 1.5× the transformation team's time, and by phase 4 in no more than 1.2×.
A route per role
Training differs by what a person does with a record: capture it, register it, analyze it, or decide with it. Each route has its own exercises and deliverables.
IV Four design decisions Each decision responds to a common way training fails.

Four decisions and the reason for each

Decision Risk it addresses What would go wrong without it

Decision A Put the clock inside the exercise

Leaders decide against a time limit from the first round, including the guided rounds.

Risk it addresses

Some knowledge only works when there is time to think. A person can explain how to verify a figure and still accept the first answer when a meeting starts in ten minutes.

Without it

Leaders would score well on tests and decide the same way as before when time was short.

Decision B Score the catch as well as the answer

The leaderboard counts whether the final decision was accurate and how often the leader caught a wrong output.

Risk it addresses

Trust can be misplaced in either direction. A score for agreement alone teaches leaders to accept the system. A score for catches alone teaches them to reject it.

Without it

Leaders would learn either to stop checking the observatory or to stop using it.

Decision C Practice on the observatory's real outputs

The questions come from each leader's own area, and the answers are the ones the observatory gave.

Risk it addresses

Sample cases teach the tool. Intuition about quality depends on local knowledge: which field is often blank, which source arrives late, and which kinds of question the agent tends to get wrong.

Without it

Leaders would know the software and still not know which of their own numbers to doubt.

Decision D Schedule the help to leave

The plan fixes the assistant's share of the operation for each phase, and a team advances on what it resolves alone.

Risk it addresses

The office could come to depend on the consulting team, and adoption would be measured while the helper was still in the room.

Without it

The office would look capable while the contract lasted. The first incident it handled alone would show how much it had learned.

Recognition. The project placed third in a city-wide competition for the best projects.

Learning gain is the share of the possible improvement that a person achieved. It is the post-test score minus the pre-test score, divided by 100 minus the pre-test score. The same test is repeated 30 days later. The plan also tracks an application rate: the share of trained people still using the tools 30–60 days later, with a target of 70%. Attendance and satisfaction are recorded, but the plan does not count them as evidence that anyone decides differently.

[1] The first two figures were measured in the diagnosis. The other three are targets from the plan, whose twelve-month horizon began in March 2026. This page reports no measured outcomes, for the plan or for the leaderboard exercises.

VI What I'd probe next Four things I would not defend as finished.

Where I think this is still weak

A practice clock is weaker than real pressure. A timed exercise has a limit but no consequences. The design assumes that practice under a time limit carries over to a committee with a director in the room. None of the plan's metrics tests that assumption directly.

Leaders can learn the game without learning the judgment. A leaderboard rewards whatever it counts. If wrong outputs turn up at a steady rate, a leader can score well by doubting at that rate, without learning to tell which outputs are wrong. The plan's own targets share the weakness, since a quota of reported incidents rewards reporting. Someone needs to review the cases behind the scores as well as the counts.

The leaderboard counts catches but not confidence. It records whether a leader caught a wrong output. It does not record how sure they were, so it cannot tell a leader who knew from one who guessed. I would ask for a confidence rating with each decision and score calibration directly. I have also not found an established method for teaching this skill to government teams, and that is the research question I want to work on next.

Practice cannot stand in for a mandate. The diagnosis found the same root cause in 4 of the 6 life-cycle stages: nobody had been formally assigned roles and responsibilities. Teams can rehearse the habits without that mandate, but the habits will not last without it.
